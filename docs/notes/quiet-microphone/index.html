A measurement

A quiet microphone breaks numbers before it breaks words

The same sentence, dictated three times. At a −41 dB peak, “paragraph one hundred and forty-two” came back as “paragraph one hundred and sixty-two”. Every other word in the sentence was fine.

That is the dangerous way round. A mangled word looks wrong, so you catch it. A wrong number looks exactly like a right one, so you don’t — and in a legal reference, an invoice or a dose, the number is the part that mattered.

What was measured

One Mac, one external microphone, one Czech sentence containing a paragraph number, an amount and two proper names, transcribed by the ElevenLabs Scribe API. Only one thing changed between the first two takes: the system input volume.

Speech normally peaks somewhere around −12 dB. At −41 dB the model was working with a signal roughly a hundred times fainter in amplitude than it expects, and the first thing to go was the distinction between two similar-sounding numbers.

This is one measurement, not a benchmark. Three takes on one machine prove that the effect exists and that it can be large; they do not tell you how often it happens on yours. What they do tell you is where to look first.

The second thing that changed the result

Raising the volume fixed the number and left the names wrong. The company name in the sentence came back as two ordinary words, and the product name was split in half. Adding both to a plain list of expected terms — and changing nothing else, at the same −25 dB level — fixed them both.

So the two fixes do different jobs and neither replaces the other. Volume protects the numbers; a vocabulary list protects the names. A better model would have done less for either than these two did together.

What to do with this

Look at the level before you blame the model. On a Mac: System Settings → Sound → Input, speak normally, and watch the meter. If it barely moves, raise the input volume. Then dictate something with a number in it and read the number.

Read numbers back. Always. Even at a good level, no engine handles digits reliably — on the same recording one run can break the amount and get the paragraph right, and the next does the reverse.

Give it your names. Proper nouns and jargon are a short list only you know, and they are what every transcription engine gets wrong.

This was measured while building NativeVoice, a free, open-source dictation app for macOS that works in 36 languages — including the ones the big tools treat as an afterthought. Its recording panel shows a live level meter for exactly this reason.
